Tectonic Earth

An interactive reconstruction of Earth's surface across 1.25 billion years — from the supercontinent Rodinia through Pangaea to the projected future supercontinent Pangaea Proxima. Land, mountains, shelves and inland seas come from published paleo-elevation data at 5-million-year steps.

How it animates

The page doesn't ship pictures of each era — it ships elevation and rainfall fields, and builds the world in a shader as you scrub. Because it interpolates the height field, a coastline genuinely migrates between keyframes instead of one image dissolving into another, so motion stays smooth at any playback speed. Relief is shaded per pixel, so terrain keeps its bite at any zoom. The future is the present-day map carried by rigid plate rotations, which is why it holds the same detail as today and leaves no seam at the present.

Layers through time

The present-day layers are surveyed data only for today. Going back, plate motion is measured from the reconstruction itself — consecutive keyframes are the same crust a few million years apart, so matching one against the next recovers how fast and which way each patch of surface travelled. Boundaries follow from that same field: crust pulling apart is a ridge, crust converging a trench. The two layers therefore always agree. Where there is no structure to match — bare abyssal plain — no motion is claimed rather than invented. Volcanism carries age windows, but a flood basalt is an eruption for a moment and a landform for far longer, so each province stays on the map for as long as it stood as high ground — the Deccan still holds up the Western Ghats, while CAMP, the largest of them all, was buried in its own rift basins almost as it erupted and is a landform essentially nowhere. Plumes are anchored in the mantle while plates slide over them, so those markers stay put and the crust moves past; provinces, which ride away on the plate, are carried back to the crust they erupted through.

Names and life

A feature that lasts 300 million years is not doing the same thing throughout, so the long-lived ones carry a description per phase of their life: the Appalachians read as a rising arc collision at 450 Ma, a Himalayan-scale range at 290, and a worn root today. Names are laid out rather than merely projected — each takes the nearest free slot to its feature, with a leader line when it has to move, and is dropped rather than drawn on top of another. Clicking a landmass also shows what its rocks preserve at that moment, and the life of the age is drawn alongside.

How the climate is derived

Biomes aren't painted on by latitude. Each frame runs a small climate model: prevailing winds (tropical easterlies, mid-latitude westerlies) carry moisture inland from the sea, drying as they go, so continental interiors go arid on their own — which is why Pangaea grows a desert heart while its coasts and Tethys shores stay monsoon-green. Air forced over mountains rains out and leaves a rain shadow behind them (the Atacama and Patagonia fall out of the model, not out of a lookup table). Vegetation is then set by rainfall against evaporative demand, so cold, dry Siberia is taiga while a subtropical plain on the same rainfall is desert.

Data sources

  • Paleogeography & elevation — Scotese & Wright (2018), PALEOMAP PaleoDEMs for the Phanerozoic (0–540 Ma), 6-arc-minute grids. CC-BY 4.0.
  • Present plate motions — NNR-MORVEL56 (Argus, Gordon & DeMets, 2011): real Euler poles drive the motion-vector field.
  • Plate boundaries — Bird (2003), PB2002 — classified as ridges, trenches and transform faults.
  • Climate & biosphere — a literature-informed model (Scotese climate zones, Haq/Hallam sea-level curves, Late-Paleozoic and Neoproterozoic glaciations). Greenhouse/icehouse anomalies are applied with polar amplification, so hothouse worlds gain ice-free poles while the tropics barely move.
  • Volcanism & impacts — the IUGS Large Igneous Provinces Commission record and Ernst's LIP compilation, the Earth Impact Database and Schmieder & Kring (2020), with ages taken from the primary geochronology where it has been revised.
  • Intervals — boundary ages follow the ICS chart v2024/12. Several differ from figures still in wide circulation: base Ordovician is 486.85 Ma, base Cretaceous 143.1 Ma.
  • Life & biomes — biome names are chosen to suit their period rather than the present: there is no grassland before the Cenozoic, and before land plants the terrestrial world is microbial crust and bare regolith. The illustrations are schematic drawings, not reconstructions.

Pre-540 Ma and future frames are authored reconstructions (real cratons rotated into supercontinent fits) and are illustrative rather than exact. Deep-time positions carry real uncertainty; treat this as a visualization of the published record, not a precise map.
