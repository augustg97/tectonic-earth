Tectonic Earth

An interactive reconstruction of Earth's surface across 1.25 billion years — from the supercontinent Rodinia through Pangaea to the projected future supercontinent Pangaea Proxima. Land, mountains, shelves and inland seas come from published paleo-elevation data at 5-million-year steps.

How it animates

The page doesn't ship pictures of each era — it ships physical fields, six of them per keyframe (elevation, rainfall, plate motion, lake depth, surface processes and ocean structure), and builds the world in a shader as you scrub. Because it interpolates the height field, a coastline genuinely migrates between keyframes instead of one image dissolving into another, so motion stays smooth at any playback speed. Relief is shaded per pixel, so terrain keeps its bite at any zoom. The future is the present-day map carried by rigid plate rotations, which is why it holds the same detail as today and leaves no seam at the present.

Layers through time

The present-day layers are surveyed data only for today. Going back, plate motion is measured from the reconstruction itself — consecutive keyframes are the same crust a few million years apart, so matching one against the next recovers how fast and which way each patch of surface travelled. Boundaries follow from that same field: crust pulling apart is a ridge, crust converging a trench. The two layers therefore always agree. Where there is no structure to match — bare abyssal plain — no motion is claimed rather than invented. Volcanism carries age windows, but a flood basalt is an eruption for a moment and a landform for far longer, so each province stays on the map for as long as it stood as high ground — the Deccan still holds up the Western Ghats, while CAMP, the largest of them all, was buried in its own rift basins almost as it erupted and is a landform essentially nowhere. Plumes are anchored in the mantle while plates slide over them, so those markers stay put and the crust moves past; provinces, which ride away on the plate, are carried back to the crust they erupted through.

The ocean floor

Two thirds of the surface is sea floor, so it is modelled rather than tinted. Each keyframe resolves that age's own spreading ridges and trenches from the plate model, and everything follows from distance to them. Depth comes from half-space cooling — new crust stands high at the axis and sinks as the square root of its age — which is why the ridges run as pale swells and the old basins lie 6 km down. The reconstruction only resolves a dozen or so ridge arcs in deep time, far too coarse to look like a spreading system, so the model synthesises the network between them: arcs are joined into continuous chains and then offset at four self-similar scales, from basin-wide ridge–transform steps down to axial crenulation, giving an axis that stays complex however far you zoom. Segments are fat in the middle and starved at the ends, so the crest shoals between nodal basins a kilometre deeper where it meets each transform. Every one of those offsets trails a fracture zone — the boundary between crust made by one segment and crust made by the next — which is where they come from here, rather than being drawn on.

Finer than the shipped grid, the floor is grown per pixel from the mechanism itself. Abyssal hills are not noise: they are tilted fault blocks, cut by normal faults at the axis and carried outward on the plate unchanged, which is why a real chart shows long parallel combing. Three self-similar fault sets reproduce that, spaced from about 24 km down to 3.6 km at the axis and widening with age as pelagic sediment mantles the short wavelengths first — so young crust is sharp, old crust subdued, and a turbidite plain against a continental margin is nearly featureless, as those plains actually are. Trenches carry an outer rise, continental slopes are cut by turbidity-current canyons, hotspot tracks smear into seamount chains along the plate's path, and oceanic plateaus rise and drown on their own curves.

Names and life

A feature that lasts 300 million years is not doing the same thing throughout, so the long-lived ones carry a description per phase of their life: the Appalachians read as a rising arc collision at 450 Ma, a Himalayan-scale range at 290, and a worn root today. Names are laid out rather than merely projected — each takes the nearest free slot to its feature, with a leader line when it has to move, and is dropped rather than drawn on top of another. Clicking a landmass also shows what its rocks preserve at that moment, and the life of the age is drawn alongside.

How the climate is derived

Biomes aren't painted on by latitude. Each frame runs a small climate model: prevailing winds (tropical easterlies, mid-latitude westerlies) carry moisture inland from the sea, drying as they go, so continental interiors go arid on their own — which is why Pangaea grows a desert heart while its coasts and Tethys shores stay monsoon-green. Air forced over mountains rains out and leaves a rain shadow behind them (the Atacama and Patagonia fall out of the model, not out of a lookup table). Vegetation is then set by rainfall against evaporative demand, so cold, dry Siberia is taiga while a subtropical plain on the same rainfall is desert.

What longitude is, and is not

The honest limit on everything above, stated with the measurement behind it. Palaeomagnetism gives a rock's palaeolatitude and its orientation, and says nothing whatever about its longitude — a reconstruction can slide every continent east or west together and fit exactly the same data. So before roughly 175 Ma, when the oldest surviving sea floor runs out and there are no more magnetic stripes to line up against, longitude is chosen by other arguments rather than measured.

How much does that matter? Measured against Ron Blakey's Deep Time Maps, an independent reconstruction by a different author on a different plate model: through the Mesozoic and Cenozoic the two are the same world to within about 5–12° of longitude. From the Devonian back they are not — at 500 Ma they place the continents up to 146° apart, and Scotese's own model moved by up to 60° between its ~2000 and 2016 editions. Meanwhile the latitudes agree throughout, including at exactly those ages: land-versus-latitude profiles match with a correlation of 0.87–0.97 across 400–525 Ma.

The uncertainty is one-dimensional, not general. How far north or south a continent was is measured; where it sat around the globe, in the Palaeozoic, is a reasoned choice. This map follows Scotese & Wright throughout — terrain, feature tracks and epicontinental seas all in that one frame, so the parts cannot disagree with each other — and a small residual against any other published reconstruction is expected rather than a defect.

Data sources

  • Paleogeography & elevation — Scotese & Wright (2018), PALEOMAP PaleoDEMs for the Phanerozoic (0–540 Ma), 6-arc-minute grids. CC-BY 4.0.
  • Feature tracks — Scotese (2016), PALEOMAP Global Plate Model (m15g60_v2d3). Labels, craters, volcanic provinces and the seeded epicontinental seas are carried on these rotations, which are the PaleoDEMs' own reference frame, so names are placed on the same Earth they are drawn on. CC-BY 4.0.
  • Present plate motions — NNR-MORVEL56 (Argus, Gordon & DeMets, 2011): real Euler poles drive the motion-vector field.
  • Plate boundaries — Bird (2003), PB2002 — classified as ridges, trenches and transform faults. Through deep time the boundaries come instead from the Merdith et al. (2021) rotation model, resolved per keyframe with pyGPlates into continuously-closing topologies.
  • Sea floor — depth from half-space cooling (Parsons & Sclater); roughness follows the von Kármán description of abyssal-hill terrain — a band-limited fractal, about 5:1 elongated, hills tens to a few hundred metres high and a few kilometres apart. Ridge segmentation, nodal basins and fracture-zone spacing are set to the observed ranges. The ridge network below the model's resolution is synthesised, not surveyed: it is a plausible spreading system consistent with the resolved arcs, not a map of one.
  • Climate & biosphere — a literature-informed model (Scotese climate zones, Late-Paleozoic and Neoproterozoic glaciations). Sea level follows one curve on purpose — Haq & Schutter (2008), which van der Meer's independent tectonic reconstruction closely agrees with. The rival backstripping family (Miller and co-workers) puts the Cretaceous high stand at roughly half this, and the two barely overlap; a 2016 review of the field is titled “a chaos of Phanerozoic eustatic curves”. Mixing them would give a curve matching neither, so the app stays inside one and says which. Greenhouse/icehouse anomalies are applied with polar amplification, so hothouse worlds gain ice-free poles while the tropics barely move.
  • Volcanism & impacts — the IUGS Large Igneous Provinces Commission record and Ernst's LIP compilation, the Earth Impact Database and Schmieder & Kring (2020), with ages taken from the primary geochronology where it has been revised.
  • Intervals — boundary ages follow the ICS chart v2024/12. Several differ from figures still in wide circulation: base Ordovician is 486.85 Ma, base Cretaceous 143.1 Ma.
  • Life & biomes — biome names are chosen to suit their period rather than the present: there is no grassland before the Cenozoic, and before land plants the terrestrial world is microbial crust and bare regolith.
  • Illustrations — most are silhouettes from PhyloPic, traced by their contributors from skeletal reconstructions and life restorations, used under CC0, Public Domain Mark or CC-BY and credited individually in the About page. A silhouette is one artist's reading of an incomplete fossil, not a photograph. Where PhyloPic has nothing to trace — an assemblage like a reef, a sedimentary structure like a stromatolite — the drawing is schematic.

Pre-540 Ma and future frames are authored reconstructions (real cratons rotated into supercontinent fits) and are illustrative rather than exact. Deep-time positions carry real uncertainty; treat this as a visualization of the published record, not a precise map.

The timeline

Rodinia to the next supercontinent

Moving through the timeline changes the whole configuration: Rodinia and a single world ocean at 1000 Ma, the Cryogenian glaciations, Pangaea's arid interior, the opening of the Atlantic, and — beyond the present — the assembly of a future supercontinent under a brightening Sun. Climate, sea level, ice, CO2 and O2 follow published estimates for each interval. The deep past and deep future are the least constrained; the future is shown as one plausible scenario, with the biosphere contracting to refugia rather than disappearing.

What you can see

A whole Earth system, modeled

Real plate motion

Ridges, trenches and transforms from the Merdith 2021 rotation model — moving continuously, keeping their names, ocean floor as active as the continents.

Climate & biomes

Winds carry moisture inland and dry the interiors; mountains cast rain shadows; vegetation follows rainfall against evaporation — so Pangaea grows a desert heart on its own.

Photoreal terrain

Per-pixel relief and hillshade, slope-bare rock on mountain flanks, and glaciers from a snowline that answers to both cold and snowfall — including the Cryogenian, where the ice reaches the equator and life holds on in thin refugia.

Rivers, marshes & deltas

Modeled drainage read from the lows of the reconstructed terrain and weighted by rainfall — channels in the valleys, marshes in wet flats, deltas at wet coasts.

Sky & sea

Deep-time events

The fossil record

Characteristic life per region and interval — flora and fauna drawn from the record, with hand-made illustrations — plus named seas, deserts, plateaus and lakes.

The future

Solar brightening and the next supercontinent — the most uncertain stretch of the timeline, shown as one plausible scenario with the land biosphere thinning toward refugia.

Method

Drainage, marshes, and deltas

Individual ancient river courses are not known, so none are drawn from a map. Drainage is instead derived from data the model already carries: the topographic lows of the reconstructed elevation field, weighted by local rainfall. Narrow incised lows are rendered as river channels, broad wet flats as marsh, and the same at a coastline as a delta plain. The resolution is valley-scale (tens of kilometres), so the result represents major drainage rather than individual tributaries, and it becomes less certain in deeper time as the underlying topography does.

As a check, the same method applied to the present-day elevation model places channels in the real Himalayan valleys draining to the Bay of Bengal. These are modeled drainage patterns, not mapped rivers.

Method

The floor of the ocean

Two thirds of the surface is sea floor, and for most of the timeline nobody has ever mapped it — the crust that recorded it has been subducted. What survives is the mechanism, and the mechanism is well understood, so that is what the model runs. Each keyframe resolves its own spreading ridges and trenches from the plate rotations, and the rest follows from distance to them: depth from half-space cooling, so new crust stands high at the axis and sinks as the square root of its age; fracture zones as the frozen wake of every transform offset; trenches with the outer rise the plate flexes into before it bends down.

The reconstruction resolves only a dozen or so ridge arcs in deep time, which is nowhere near enough to read as a spreading system, so the network between them is synthesised rather than interpolated — arcs joined into continuous chains, then offset at four self-similar scales from basin-wide ridge–transform steps down to axial crenulation. Finer still, below anything the shipped grid could hold, the floor is grown per pixel from the fault mechanism itself: abyssal hills are tilted fault blocks, cut at the axis and carried outward on the plate, which is why real fabric combs in long parallel lines rather than dappling. Sediment then buries the short wavelengths first, so young crust near a ridge is sharp and an old plain against a continental margin is nearly featureless.

This is a plausible spreading system consistent with the resolved arcs, not a survey of one. The individual ridge segments and fracture zones are modelled, in the way the modelled rivers are: the pattern is real, the particular line is not.

Under the hood

How it's built

The engine

Ships six physical fields per 5-Myr keyframe — elevation, rainfall, plate motion, lake depth, surface processes and ocean structure; a GLSL shader interpolates them and recomputes temperature, relief, biomes, water and sky per pixel — nothing is a pre-baked picture.

Ocean structure

Each age's ridges and trenches are resolved from the plate model, then a spreading network is synthesised between them at four self-similar scales. Depth follows half-space cooling; fracture zones fall out of the segmentation; abyssal hills are grown per pixel as tilted fault blocks and mantled by sediment with age.

Plate motion

The Merdith et al. (2021) full-plate rotation model, resolved with pyGPlates into continuously-closing plate topologies and classified boundaries.

Paleogeography

Scotese & Wright PALEOMAP paleo-elevation models for 0–540 Ma; an authored craton composite deeper in the Precambrian; the present terrain rotated forward for the future.

Climate

Temperature from latitude, elevation and an era anomaly; rainfall from wind-driven moisture transport, orographic rain shadow and Budyko humidity — calibrated to published GMST, CO2 and O2 curves.

Sun & future

Solar luminosity from the Gough (1981) standard model (−8% at 1000 Ma, brighter ahead); the deep future calibrated to Farnsworth et al. (2024) on the same Pangaea Ultima reconstruction.

Events & life

Impact structures vetted against the Impact Earth database and Schmieder & Kring (2020); fossil-record biota per region and interval, each event and label written up by hand.
